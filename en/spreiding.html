Plot 3 — Spread

Which product lines hang on a single customer, and which customers buy more than one machine?

A customer-revenue table tells you what a customer does. A product-revenue table tells you what a product line does. The question “how dependent are we on a customer-product-line combination?” falls between the two tables. Here it comes together at a glance.

One bubble per project. Customers vertically, grouped by sub-sector (Bakery, Specialty, Protein, Dairy, Sauces), and within a sub-sector by revenue descending. Machine types horizontally, also by revenue descending. Bubble size = revenue of the project. Open circle = 2024, filled circle = 2025. Color = margin band: red < 10%, ink 10–20%, green ≥ 20%. Empty cells are customer-machine combinations that do not occur.

With your own data: the customer-product links you already have in your records anyway (Exact, AFAS, Ridder, Isah, Plan-de-CAMpagne). Every combination becomes a bubble. The concentration risk, product lines tied to one customer or customers leaning on one product line, jumps out at a glance. The empty cells tell you where you are not, sometimes deliberately, sometimes a missed opportunity.

How to read this
  • One column with one bubble: product line hung on a single customer. Concentration risk, especially if that one bubble is small or red. Stretch wrapper W8 at Verlinden is the sharpest example: one product line, one customer, red in both years.
  • Vertical stack in the same cell: open and filled on top of each other means a repeat order. Same customer, same machine, two years. Says something about the depth of the relationship.
  • One row with multiple bubbles: customer buys more than one machine type. Often a sign of a deeper relationship, or that the customer takes the whole line from you.
  • One column with many bubbles: workhorse product line that you sell to many customers. Tray loader T15 spreads across four customers and is therefore less dependent on any single relationship.

The cross table is deliberately thin, 22 bubbles across 112 cells. That emptiness is exactly the information: it shows where you are not, not just where you are. Strong leadership topic: which empty cells are a missed opportunity and which are deliberate positioning?

How to bring this to leadership

A portfolio conversation calls for two things: a picture of where you hang and a picture of where you are moving. This matrix does the first. The direct question for leadership: which customers depend on one product line, and which product lines hang on one customer? Verlinden Vis on Stretch wrapper is the sharpest example, two years in a row red and only there.

The sub-sector grouping makes a second layer visible: all five Bakery customers lean on Tray loader, Packing robot, or Labeling. That is a coherent segment that can be served with a few product lines. Dairy hangs on Packaging line (large but thin). Protein sits entirely on Sleeve and Stretch wrapper, the same two types under the heaviest margin pressure, a pattern that gets lost line by line in a customer report.

Recommendation that can roll out of this plot: not "broaden to more customers" as a general course, but a targeted look at which sub-sectors are underserved (herbs, canned goods) and whether there are customers outside the current top who would fit an empty cell with good margin.
